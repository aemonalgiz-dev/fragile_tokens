# Fragile Tokens

### Ordinary tokens that pass every glitch probe, and fail anyway once there are words around them

Jeffrey R. Gordon · September 2026

---

This paper settles a debt. Every method for finding glitch tokens, from the first list of anomalous strings in GPT-2 to the detectors published this year, asks its question of one token at a time: show the model a vocabulary entry alone, ask it to repeat it, and label the ones that fail. That is a reasonable question and the answer to it is correct. The debt is that people using these models keep describing something the question cannot see. A token behaves when it stands on its own and breaks when there are words around it; a reasoning model "struggles to identify" it and "conflates" it with another script; a single instance does nothing while a longer input produces confident nonsense. Two readings of those reports are available, that glitch behaviour is a matter of which tokens are *combined*, or that it is a matter of which token sits in what *company*, and this paper tests both on open weights.

The model throughout is a seven-billion-parameter instruction-tuned model, OLMo-2-1124-7B-Instruct, chosen because an independent group published behavioural glitch labels for its base model: 3,171 tokens tested with a repetition probe, 442 verified as failing. Where a token is called healthy below it means that group tested it and it passed. Where a number is quoted, it came from greedy decoding on that model at commit `470b1fba`, and every failing case is stored with its context and its probabilities so that nothing here has to be taken on description.

---

## Part 1. What a Single Probe Flatters

Take the English word `according`. It is token 4184 in a vocabulary of a hundred thousand, which places it among the most frequent pieces the tokenizer knows, and it is the canonical token for that word: decode it, encode the string again, and the same id comes back. Ask the model to repeat it, in the few-shot form every detector uses — *Repeat the text exactly. Text: according. Copy:* — and the log-probability of the correct answer is −0.001. It copies at 0.999. By the standard that has defined a glitch token since 2023, `according` is as healthy as a token gets.

Now put it in a sentence. Not a real one; a sequence of ordinary common words drawn at random, so that nothing about the surroundings is chosen, with `according` dropped into one slot. *deliver rel behind according sent floor wrong.* Ask the model to repeat that, and at the moment it should produce `according` it produces `sent` instead, with probability 1.000 and an entropy of 0.00 bits, and then continues the copy correctly from there as if the word had never been in the text. Do it again with different random words around it, forty-eight times, and it is deleted in forty-four of them. In the other four it copies at 0.999.

Nothing about the token changed between those two paragraphs. What changed is that the first measurement was made in the one context where induction has a single candidate to copy, and the second was made in the contexts a token actually lives in. A score on a token alone measures whether the model can reproduce it when nothing competes; whether that reproduction survives company is a different question, and it is the second question a system that assembles prompts by machine is asking every time it runs.

The copy probe used here is worth fixing before anything is built on it, because the obvious way to build it is wrong. A prompt assembled as a string — the context typed out with the token spliced into the text — passes through the tokenizer, and a leading space re-segments about nine tokens in ten into different ids regardless of whether the token is healthy: verified glitch tokens survive a preceding space 8.3% of the time and healthy tokens 9.3%. Build the prompt that way and the experiment measures the tokenizer. Every prompt below is therefore assembled from token ids directly, and the score is the teacher-forced log-probability of the target id at the position where the copy should produce it. The reported rule that these tokens "cannot be preceded by a space or a bracket but can follow a newline" is real, and it is exactly the pre-tokenizer's boundary set, and it holds for nearly every token in the vocabulary; it explains the usage pattern and says nothing about which tokens are glitchy.

Read Part 1 for what it does not say. One word failing in forty-four contexts is a specimen, not a rate, and a specimen chosen because it is striking says nothing about how many tokens behave this way. Part 4 measures that. What the specimen does establish is that the single probe and the in-context probe can disagree completely on a common canonical token, which is enough to make the rest of the paper necessary.

## Part 2. What Happens Instead of an Error

Before measuring how often the in-context failure happens, it is worth being exact about what it is, because the reports describe it as confusion and it is not.

Thirty-two verified glitch tokens and thirty-two healthy controls were each spliced into the model's chat template after *What is this token? Identify it exactly, then explain step by step what it means*, and the model was allowed 256 tokens of answer. Two things the reports predicted were checked first. Neither happened. The gap in next-token entropy between glitch-seeded and control-seeded answers is +0.27 bits at step 8 and −0.29 bits at step 255; it wanders around zero and does not grow with length. And the answers seeded by different glitch tokens do not converge on some shared confused state: the alignment between chains, measured as the mean pairwise cosine of their centred final-layer states, decays from 0.016 to 0.0001 for glitch seeds and from 0.017 to 0.0002 for controls. There is no basin.

What happens is that the model answers a different question. Shown `.XtraLayout` it wrote that *the token "layui" refers to a lightweight, modular, and flexible JavaScript framework primarily used for building web interfaces*. Shown ` Hexatrigesimal` it explained that *the token "Calculus" refers to a branch of mathematics that deals with the study of change*. Shown the control ` woman` it wrote that *the token "woman" is a noun in the English language* and went on correctly. Across the thirty-two glitch seeds the model reproduced the token it had been shown zero times; across the thirty-two controls, seventeen. Where it substituted, it did so at 0.85 to 1.3 bits of entropy, which is the confidence of a model that knows the answer, and it went on to give a step-by-step explanation of the wrong token in every case. In the experiment of Part 3, where a trivially answerable question follows the token, the rate at which the model completed that task was 1.000 in every arm. It does not stop. It does not hedge. It works, on substituted content.

That is the correct statement of the hazard, and it is why detectors built on entropy do not see it: the signal a guardrail would watch for is not emitted. It is also worth asking whether the model has lost the token or merely failed to say it, because those call for different remedies. On a smaller model with the same kind of labels, Pythia-1.4b, each of thirty-six verified glitch tokens and 1,088 controls was placed at the end of eight different contexts and its hidden state read at every layer. The question put to each layer was whether the token could be picked out of a pool of 1,124 candidates by matching its state in one context to its state in another, with the shared component of each context removed first. At every layer from the embedding to the twelfth, glitch tokens were identified 1.000 of the time and controls 0.988 to 1.000; at the last layer, 0.880 against 0.874. The token's identity arrives at the top of the network as intact for a glitch token as for a healthy one. What fails is the step that turns that state back into a word. A logit lens at the same positions shows the model *continuing* glitch tokens correctly — `idepress` is followed by `ant` at every layer, as running text would have it — while being unable to *name* them. Pretraining on prose rewards the first and never asks for the second.

Keep this distinction beside everything that follows. A glitch failure is not a token the model does not know; it is a token the model cannot refer to, and the refusal is silent.

## Part 3. Two Tokens Do Not Conspire

The compositional reading of the reports, put as sharply as a collaborator put it: in `[1, 231, 885, 9911, 1112, 231]` the *repetition* of 231 causes the glitch, and in `[1, 231, 885, 9911, 9999, 1922, 1013]` the *co-occurrence* of 885 and 1013 does, the members individually healthy and as far apart as you like. Earlier work on this project had tested adjacent pairs and contiguous runs of tokens and found nothing, which was the wrong object; distance was never the variable there. So the question was put properly.

A carrier is a sequence of ordinary filler words with two slots in it at positions 2 and 2 + d, and for an ordered pair of tokens (a, b) four versions of it are scored: both slots holding control words, a in the first slot and a control in the second, a control in the first and b in the second, and a and b together. Each absent token is replaced by a control rather than removed, so the length and every other position are held fixed, and the two controls are different words so that the all-control version is not itself a repetition. The interaction is the fourth score minus the second minus the third plus the first: whatever damage the pair does that neither token does alone. Distance d ran over 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64 and 128, with two independent filler sequences at each, and carriers at different distances share a prefix so that distance is not confounded with what the filler happens to say. The pool was sixty-four tokens, first all individually clean and then thirty-two clean with thirty-two *weak* — tokens that copy alone only some of the time, so that a combination has room to push them over. Each run scored 63,014 carriers.

Four thousand ordered pairs is four thousand simultaneous tests on a difference of four noisy numbers, and without a null that is honest about that it is a machine for producing false discoveries. So the null was fixed before any real data was scored. Within each carrier the matrix of pair scores is fitted additively, a row effect for a and a column effect for b, and the residual is the interaction; residuals are permuted within the carrier, averaged across carriers, and the per-pair p-values are corrected by Benjamini–Hochberg at five percent with a maximum-statistic family-wise test alongside. The threshold for calling the screen positive is not a fixed number of pairs but the ninety-fifth percentile of the rejection count over twenty datasets simulated from the additive fit with the real data's own noise. The first version of this null was wrong, and it is worth saying how, because a synthetic check found it before the data did. On matrices where one token in eight was three times noisier than the rest — which real vocabularies are — the pooled permutation gave five to eighteen false rejections and a family-wise p of 0.003 in every run, since a noisy token's pairs have large residuals against a null calibrated to the average. Standardising each residual by the robust scale of its row and column before permuting gave zero false rejections in ten runs of ten. That version is the one the screen used.

The screen came back empty. In the clean pool, one pair of 4,032 was significant, against a matched-null ninety-fifth percentile of 4; in the mixed pool, one of 4,032 against 14. The averages say the same thing without leaning on the test's power, and they are the number to remember. Among pairs of two clean tokens the mean interaction was +0.0011 log-probability with a standard error of 0.0004. Among pairs of two weak tokens, whose joint score was −0.313 — twelve times worse than the clean pairs, all of it inherited from what each weak token does alone — the mean interaction was +0.0076 with a standard error of 0.0027. Slightly positive, in every cell type, at every distance from 2 to 128: two weak tokens copy fractionally *better* together than the sum of their separate damage would predict. The positive-control glitch tokens placed in single slots dropped to between −3 and −6.5 against a clean baseline of −0.02, so the scorer sees a real effect when there is one to see.

Repetition, the first of the two reported mechanisms, turned out to run the other way. Along the diagonal of the clean pool, where the same token fills both slots, the mean interaction was +0.0066 with a permutation p of 0.000, and it grew with distance, from +0.005 at two positions apart to +0.018 at 128. A healthy token repeated at a distance is easier to copy the second time because its first occurrence is in the context; that is induction doing its job. In the mixed pool a handful of tokens did fail when repeated, `Tools` at −1.39 in one filler sequence at distance 128, and the same token in the other filler sequence at the same distance read −0.000. Where repetition hurt, it hurt in one context and not the other.

That last observation is the one the screen was actually for. Even in the clean pool, 453 of 57,344 carriers failed outright, and they were not spread around: five tokens accounted for 47% of them, `.Ver` failing 111 times in the first slot and `Sentence` 118 times in the second. They also did not recur. At each distance the two filler sequences were checked for carriers that failed in both, and the counts were 3, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0, 2. At distance 4 one filler produced 68 failures and the other produced 1. The failures factor as a fragile token in a hostile context, which is exactly the structure an additive model absorbs and an interaction term cannot see. Two tokens do not conspire. One token, in the wrong company, fails.

## Part 4. Fragility

So the object to measure is not a pair. It is a token, and the quantity is how often ordinary company breaks it.

Forty-eight contexts were built by drawing common, individually clean words at random — twelve each of lengths 8, 16, 32 and 64, with a slot chosen at random somewhere inside — and 2,098 tokens were placed in every one of them: 1,998 sampled across the vocabulary with the id range stratified into thirds, since uniform sampling of ids lands mostly in the rare tail, and 100 of the verified glitch tokens as a reference. The cell is the copy log-probability at the slot; a cell fails below −0.5, which is a probability of 0.61; and a token's **fragility** is the fraction of the forty-eight contexts it fails in. Because every token meets the same forty-eight contexts, a difference in fragility between two tokens is a difference between the tokens. A second bank was then built independently — 6,273 tokens in 24 new contexts — to see whether any of it held.

The first thing the matrix answers is where the variation lives. Write each cell as a grand mean plus a token effect plus a context effect plus whatever is left, and the shares of variance come out as 0.952 for the token, 0.001 for the context and 0.047 for the remainder over all tokens on the log-probability scale; over the clean-looking tokens alone, 0.717, **0.000**, and 0.282. No context is hostile in general. The most hostile of the forty-eight fails 19% of tokens and the friendliest fails 10%, and the failure rate of clean-looking tokens is flat from eight words to sixty-four: 0.005, 0.008, 0.008, 0.008. The 28% that is neither token nor context is a token failing in *its* contexts, and a held-out test says the same from the other side. Predicting whether a given token fails in a given context it has not been scored in, the token's fragility on other contexts gives an AUC of 0.94, the context's hostility on other tokens gives 0.56, and the similarity between the token and the words around it adds nothing to either. The sensitivity is real and it is the token's. Which context sets it off is not something the geometry of the context predicts.

The second thing it answers is how common this is, and the answer is that it is rare and it is not nothing. Among the 1,998 random tokens the mean fragility was 0.090, and 17.5% failed in a tenth or more of contexts, most of those being tokens the single probe would also have flagged. Among the 100 verified glitch tokens the mean was 0.993; every one failed in every context but a handful, and none of them passed the single probe, which is the measure agreeing with the label it was checked against. Among the 873 tokens that *did* pass the single probe — copied alone at better than 0.9 — the mean fragility was 0.007, 92.1% never failed once, and **1.6% failed in a tenth or more of ordinary contexts**. The second bank, with 1,888 clean-looking tokens in twenty-four new contexts, gave 1.5%. About one token in sixty that every existing detector would certify fails in one context in ten. It is a stable property of the token rather than an accident of the bank: fragility measured on the even-numbered contexts predicts fragility on the odd-numbered contexts at an AUC of 0.996, and `according`, at 0.92 in the first bank, was 0.92 in the second.

The fourteen fragile clean-looking tokens in the first bank were checked for the boring explanation, which is that they are not the tokens they appear to be. A vocabulary can hold two ids that decode to the same string, one canonical and one a leftover of a decomposed accent, and the leftover would copy after *Copy:* and lose to its twin in a real sentence. All fourteen round-trip, decode to encode, to the same id; all fourteen are in Unicode's composed form; all fourteen are classed `OK` by the labelling group's own taxonomy. They are ordinary tokens.

Here are some of them, with the worst context each met and what the model produced in its place.

| token | id | alone | fragility | in its worst context the model produced |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `' according'` | 4184 | −0.001 | 0.92 | `' sent'`, the next word, at 1.000 — deleted, copy continues |
| `' pueden'` | 41604 | −0.030 | 0.62 | `' pena'` at 0.775 |
| `' abbiamo'` | 95396 | −0.020 | 0.60 | `' abdom'` and then *abdominoplasty* |
| `' sólo'` | 53288 | −0.032 | 0.48 | `' só'` at 0.892 |
| `' 查询'` | 81628 | −0.015 | 0.46 | `' QUERY'` at 0.970 |
| `' который'` | 98851 | −0.017 | 0.71 | `' что'` |
| `' getSystemService'` | 92476 | −0.053 | 0.38 | `'SystemService'` at 0.30 |
| `' due'` | — | −0.000 | 0.42 | deleted; worst cell −23.8 |

Four things happen, and they are the same four things as Part 2 wearing plainer clothes. The token is **deleted**, the model emitting the next word of the context and continuing as though the slot were empty. It is **substituted** with a neighbour, Spanish for Spanish, an identifier for a similar identifier. It is **truncated** to a piece of itself. Or it is **translated**: the Chinese for *query* comes back as the English word `QUERY` at 0.970, and the Russian for *which* comes back as the Russian for *what*. Every one of these is confident, and every one reverses when the filler words change; the same `according` that is deleted at 1.000 in three contexts copies at 1.000 in a fourth.

Two regularities in the fragile set are recorded here without having been tested, because they are the obvious next experiment. The two catastrophic English cases, `according` and `due`, are words whose next token is almost always `to`, and several of the fragile connectives — Italian `mentre`, German `allerdings`, Polish `może`, Russian `который` — are likewise words that strongly predict what follows them. It may be that fragility is the peakedness of a token's continuation prior: a word the model will not copy into a slot where the word that must come next is absent. And a deletion at probability 1.000 and entropy zero is what a copy-suppression head, the circuit described in 2023 that suppresses re-emission of an earlier token when the prediction is confident, would look like from outside. The rest of the fragile set is the rare tail of an English-heavy instruction mix, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, German, Polish, Chinese and Russian words, Android and Java identifiers, which is what one would expect if rarity were most of the story and Part 5 is where that expectation is checked.

Every failing cell and a control cell for each of these tokens, and for twenty verified glitch tokens beside them, is stored: the exact context and slot, the five most probable emissions with their probabilities, the entropy at that position, the probability the model gave the correct token, the greedy continuation, and a label for which of the four things happened, under a header recording the model, its commit, its precision, its depth and width and vocabulary, and the library versions. There are 228 such records. The claim in this paper that the failures are confident is a claim you can read off them.

## Part 5. What the Embedding Already Knows

If fragility is a property of the token, the question a practitioner asks next is whether it can be read off the model without running it. Three tiers of prediction were tried, kept apart because they cost different things.

The first tier is static: features computed from the two embedding matrices and nothing else. The norm of the token's input row and its distance from the centroid; the same two for its output row; how close its nearest neighbour sits and how dense its neighbourhood is; how strongly the input row excites its own output row along the direct path, with the final layer norm folded in, and the margin by which it does so over the best competitor. The second tier is dynamic: the token's own representation on half of the contexts — how much its hidden state at the slot varies from context to context, how large that state is, how closely it still points at its own unembedding row — used to predict its failures on the other half, so that nothing predicts itself. The third tier is not geometry at all: simply measure copy on half the contexts and predict the other half. Every geometric feature had to beat a set of surface features that any spreadsheet could supply — the token's id, which in a byte-pair vocabulary is roughly its frequency rank, its length in characters, whether it begins with a space, whether it is alphabetic, whether it is ASCII — because a geometric feature that only knows a token is rare has not earned its name.

The target was a token failing in a tenth or more of the held-out contexts among the clean-looking ones. In the first bank that was thirteen tokens, and thirteen positives is too few to settle anything; the second bank gave twenty-eight, and it is the second bank that decides. On it, the single static features are modest. Distance of the output row from the centroid gives an AUC of 0.678 with a bootstrap interval from 0.55 to 0.79; the direct-path self-score gives 0.655, from 0.56 to 0.75; the input row's norm and centroid distance give about 0.60. The dynamic tier's best single feature, the norm of the slot state across the training contexts, gives 0.864, from 0.78 to 0.92. Simply measuring copy on the training half gives 0.997.

Combined by a cross-validated logistic regression and scored out of fold, the tiers separate. The surface features alone reach 0.711, from 0.58 to 0.83. Static geometry reaches 0.862, from 0.77 to 0.94. The dynamic tier reaches 0.823 and static with dynamic together 0.899, from 0.82 to 0.96. Two intervals overlapping says nothing about their difference, so the difference was bootstrapped directly, in pairs over the same tokens: static geometry beats the surface tier by +0.151, with an interval from +0.055 to +0.258; the dynamic tier by +0.112, from +0.024 to +0.216; the two together by +0.188, from +0.086 to +0.302. None of the intervals contains zero. The first bank's 0.845 on thirteen tokens, which could have been thirteen tokens' worth of luck, reproduces at 0.862 on twenty-eight.

Read this for what it does not say. No single geometric feature exceeds 0.68; the 0.86 belongs to the combination, and the intervals on the improvements are wide, +0.06 to +0.26, so the sign is established and the size is not. And the features that carry the signal are, in the end, rarity with a mechanism attached: the output row that sits far from the centroid and excites its own readout weakly is the row that was seldom the target during training and so was seldom moved. That mechanism is Part 6. What the tiers do establish is a ladder a practitioner can climb as far as they can afford. From the weights alone, with no forward pass, fragility can be pre-screened at 0.86. With a dozen contexts' worth of forward passes the token's own representation lifts that to 0.90. With a dozen contexts' worth of copy scores it is 0.99, and there is no reason to guess.

## Part 6. Where the Signal Comes From, and Where It Does Not Go

The mechanism behind Part 5 is not this paper's. That the embedding rows of rare tokens are starved of gradient — receiving it chiefly as softmax negatives, in a direction they share with every other rare row — was shown in 2022 and is the premise two architectures published this year are built to counter, one by decoupling input from output representations and one by re-injecting the token's identity at every layer under the name of the Rare Token Problem. What the checkpoint phase of this project adds is measurement on real pretraining runs with behaviourally labelled glitch tokens, and it agrees. Decomposing each update to the embedding matrix into the component along the mean direction of that update and the remainder, verified glitch rows receive the shared component in comparable or greater measure than healthy rows while their token-specific remainder is suppressed to between 0.15 and 0.74 of healthy, across seven model lineages on three corpora, and in a LLaMA-architecture model sharing no ancestry with the others at 0.573. Under weight tying the picture reverses in the way the mechanism says it must, the shared component rising to 1.17 to 1.23 times healthy, since a tied row is a softmax negative at every step and receives dense gradient it would not otherwise see. The output row that Part 5 reads fragility from is the row that was rarely the answer.

The same decomposition explains something that looked like a finding and was not. Instruction tuning, preference tuning and reinforcement leave a large share of the vocabulary untouched, 23% of rows receiving no token-specific update across one model's final stage and 35% across another's, and the first reading of that was that post-training must be manufacturing new glitch tokens out of rows it ignores. Across eight stage series it does not, and the decomposition says why: the total movement of a row and its token-specific movement agree to three decimal places in every family, 0.550 against 0.550, 0.277 against 0.277, 0.030 against 0.030. Post-training has essentially no shared drift. A row it never touches is left exactly where pretraining put it, and being ignored is not being damaged; being dragged is.

There is a second place the signal does not go, and it is the one this paper most wanted it to. Confident substitution on information the model still has is a hallucination in miniature — fluent, wrong, a plausible neighbour of the truth, no uncertainty shown — and the natural hope is that token fragility is a mechanism for one class of real hallucination, the API name that almost exists, the author with a syllable swapped. So it was tested rather than suggested. 1,255 real entities — 1,100 identifiers taken from Python's standard library and from torch and transformers as they sit in the interpreter, spanning two to six tokens, and 155 real place names, people and substances across several scripts — were used in a natural task, *write one line of Python that imports or calls* `X`, *write one sentence that mentions* `Y`, and the answer was scored on the entity's final component as exact, a near miss with fuzzy similarity of 0.75 or better, or a miss. The model reproduced the real API name exactly 82.5% of the time, mangled it 1.7% of the time — `curses.BUTTON5_PRESSED` became `C_BUTTON5_PRESSED`, `torch.BoolTensor` became `bool_tensor` — and in the remaining 15.7% wrote a bare `import` line and never used the name at all, which is avoidance and not hallucination; proper nouns it reproduced 99.4% of the time. Every constituent token of every entity had been placed in the second fragility bank on purpose, so each entity carried the fragility of its parts. Added to a model that already knew the entity's rarity, its constituents' single-probe scores, and the model's own familiarity with the whole string as teacher-forced log-probability, fragility changed the out-of-fold AUC for getting the name wrong by −0.001, with an interval from −0.008 to +0.006. The first version of this scorer compared the whole dotted path and counted `numpy.X` written as `np.X` as a near miss; it is not, and that is why the analysis was rerun on the final component, and both versions reached the same verdict. Fragility and hallucination share a cause, which is rarity, and on this evidence they share nothing else. The resemblance describes what the failure looks like. It does not say why models hallucinate.

## Part 7. Keep in Mind

The behavioural results are on one model, with a second used only for the layer-by-layer identity check. The interaction screen and the fragility matrix run unchanged on any model for which a set of behavioural labels exists, and a second family is the obvious next run.

Copy log-probability is a stand-in for the reasoning-mode behaviour that the reports are about. It was chosen because it is continuous and deterministic, which an experiment of four thousand pairs or six thousand tokens needs, and because every generation inspected alongside it agreed with it. An exploratory attempt to confirm the screen's top pairs in reasoning mode was confounded — three tokens dominated the selected pairs, and half the matched controls dropped the anchor token — and is reported as inconclusive rather than as anything else.

The intervals in Part 5 exclude zero and are wide. The geometry's advantage over rarity is established in sign and not in size.

The contexts in Part 4 are random common words rather than natural text, and the finding that no context is hostile is a finding about that bank.

Which context breaks a given token is not explained. The continuation-prior and copy-suppression hypotheses of Part 4 are each a single forward pass per token to test.

---

Glitch behaviour is not compositional and it is not an information loss. Two tokens at a distance do not conspire, and a repeated healthy token copies better rather than worse. A glitch token's identity reaches the last layer of the network, and the readout substitutes a plausible neighbour anyway, confidently, and the model finishes whatever it was asked to do on the wrong content. What does vary with the words around a token is a property of the token: about one in sixty of the tokens that pass every single-context probe fails to copy in a tenth or more of ordinary contexts — deleted, swapped, truncated or translated — with the trigger unpredictable but the fact stable, replicable across context banks, and readable from the output embedding better than rarity alone can read it. A detector that scores tokens one at a time is measuring the wrong object for a system that assembles text by machine. Fragility costs one forward pass per token per context, two dozen contexts suffice, it holds still long enough to be measured once per model, and it finds the `according`s.

## What did not survive

Each of these was asserted at some point and then killed by a control, and several would have been the paper had the control not been run. They are here so that the reader knows the shape of the ground that was cleared.

That the "no space before it" rule marks a glitch token: it marks the pre-tokenizer's boundary, and healthy tokens obey it at the same rate. That adjacencies the tokenizer can never produce cause damage when fed as ids: they were easier to copy than the same tokens in the producible order, +0.94 with an interval from +0.61 to +1.26. That runs of individually perfect tokens interact: reproduction falls from 1.000 at one token to 0.648 at eight, but independence from the two-token rate predicts 0.354, so the fall is less than additive, not more. That the substitute a model produces can be read from the direct path E_in·E_out: top-one agreement 0.003. That the substitutions are spelling completions: 11% of them. That reasoning length amplifies the damage, or that glitch chains share a basin: Part 2. That the persistent homology of a generation trajectory separates glitch from healthy seeds: best AUC 0.597 on sixty-four chains, underpowered. That post-training creates new glitch tokens: real movement in one lineage and boundary jitter in five others, and Part 6 says why it had to fail. That the fragile tokens are Unicode variants: all canonical. That fragility explains near-miss hallucination on real entities: Part 6.
